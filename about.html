Method

How a claim becomes a file

Arı Kaynak exists because most viral health content is compressed past the point of being accurate. Here's exactly how we check it.

"Experts make claims. Evidence tests them."

Arı Kaynak investigates public claims made by qualified experts — professors, physicians, researchers. We don't accept or reject a claim because of who said it. The authority of the speaker tells you who to ask; it doesn't answer whether the claim is accurate. Only the evidence does that. If strong evidence supports what an expert said, the file says so. If it doesn't, the file says that instead. The goal is investigation, not debunking — most files here end up somewhere between those two poles.

The research chain

Every file follows the same underlying sequence, even when the section headings vary with the content:

  1. The claim — stated as it actually circulated, not strengthened or softened.
  2. Original source — the actual video, post, or statement the claim came from.
  3. Scientific or official source — the underlying study, trial, dataset, or regulatory record.
  4. Evidence — what that source actually found, in plain language.
  5. Limitations — sample size, study design, what the source doesn't prove.
  6. Evidence strength — a 1–5 rating of the underlying source (see below).
  7. Verdict — how closely the claim's own wording matches the evidence.

1. We start with the claim, not the conclusion

Every file begins with something that's actually circulating — a video, a post, a headline — quoted as closely as fair use allows, so you can see exactly what was said before we say anything about it.

2. We find the original source

We trace the claim back to the underlying study, trial, or dataset — not to another article that cited it. If we can't find a credible primary source, we say so in the file instead of filling the gap with plausible-sounding text.

3. We compare, not just summarize

Each file separates what the research actually supports from what got added in translation — a stronger verb, a dropped caveat, an animal result presented as a human one. That gap is the whole point of the file.

4. Every file gets a stamp

Supported, Mostly Supported, Partly Supported, Misleading, or Unsupported. The stamp evaluates the exact wording of the claim against the exact finding of the source — not whether the underlying science or topic is legitimate. A claim can come from a strong study and still be stamped Mostly Supported if the claim's own phrasing (a stronger verb, an absolute like "no matter what" or "proven") reaches slightly further than what was actually measured.

5. We rate the evidence on a 5-point scale

Every file lists an evidence type (randomized controlled trial, cohort study, meta-analysis, animal study, position statement, regulatory approval, etc.) and an evidence strength rating:

  • 5/5 — Very High — regulatory-grade evidence: Phase 3 trials behind an official approval, or equivalent.
  • 4/5 — High — large, well-controlled studies, systematic reviews, or expert-consensus position statements with graded evidence.
  • 3/5 — Moderate — a single well-run trial or cohort study, not yet replicated at scale.
  • 2/5 — Limited — early-stage evidence, a narrative review proposing a conceptual shift, or animal studies extrapolated to humans.
  • 1/5 — Very Limited — preliminary, indirect, or substantially constrained by study design.

These ratings describe the source we cite, not our confidence in our own writing. A prestigious journal doesn't automatically raise the score, and a controversial topic doesn't automatically lower it. We don't upgrade a rating because a finding is convenient or interesting.

6. When the evidence is weak, we say so

If a claim traces back to a single small study, an animal model, or a preprint that hasn't been peer-reviewed, the file says that explicitly rather than presenting it with the same confidence as a large randomized trial. If we can't find a credible primary source at all, we don't publish a verdict — we publish that gap.

Editorial ethics

We don't attack, ridicule, or personally discredit the person making a claim — we critique the claim and the evidence, not the speaker. Evidence not supporting a claim is never treated as evidence of misconduct. We don't selectively cite sources to manufacture a predetermined verdict, and we don't use absolute language like "false" unless the evidence genuinely justifies that level of certainty.

Who's behind this

Arı Kaynak is an independently run publication. It is not affiliated with any pharmaceutical, supplement, or wellness brand, and does not accept payment in exchange for a specific verdict. The English edition is written for an international audience, not exclusively a Turkish one.

Corrections

If you believe a file misrepresents a source, mislabels a study type, or contains an error, you can open an issue on our public GitHub repository. Substantive corrections are noted directly on the affected file rather than removed quietly.
