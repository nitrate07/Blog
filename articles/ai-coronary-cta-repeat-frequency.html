Should AI-Enhanced Coronary CT Angiography Be Repeated Every Year?

Two separate claims are packed into this one: that AI-assisted "virtual angiography" is a real, current technology, and that repeating it every year isn't automatically the right call. Both check out — with real nuance.

Evidence strengthHigh4/5 ●●●●○

This file poses a question rather than a single factual claim: is AI-enhanced coronary CT angiography ("virtual angiography") a real technology, and should it be repeated annually? Both halves are checkable against real, current medical evidence.

Is "AI-supported virtual angiography" real?

Yes. Coronary CT angiography (CCTA) — a non-invasive CT scan of the heart's arteries, sometimes called "virtual" angiography because it avoids the catheter used in invasive angiography — now commonly incorporates AI-based quantitative plaque analysis (AI-QCT). This software automatically measures plaque volume and composition from the CT images, and its results correlate strongly with more invasive imaging methods like intravascular ultrasound in published validation studies. This isn't an emerging curiosity: the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services assigned AI-based quantitative plaque analysis a Category I CPT code effective 2026, and multiple private insurers began covering it in late 2025 — both signs of a technology that has moved from research into standard clinical infrastructure.

What the guidelines say about repeating it

The current multi-society guidance — the 2023 ACC/AHA/ASE/ASNC/ASPC/HFSA/HRS/SCAI/SCCT/SCMR/STS Multimodality Appropriate Use Criteria for the Detection and Risk Assessment of Chronic Coronary Disease — is the standing reference document for when cardiac imaging, including CCTA, is and isn't appropriate. It doesn't treat "once a year" as a default. Consistent with earlier versions of this same multi-society framework, routine repeat testing on a fixed calendar schedule, without new or worsening symptoms or a meaningful change in cardiovascular risk, is generally rated as inappropriate or of uncertain benefit.

In practice, this means the decision to repeat coronary CT angiography should be driven by a clinical trigger — new chest pain, a significant change in risk factors, or findings from the prior scan that specifically call for follow-up imaging — not simply the passage of twelve months.

Why this isn't just caution for its own sake

There are real reasons the guidelines discourage routine repeat scanning: CT angiography involves ionizing radiation and contrast dye, both of which carry cumulative and individual risks. Unnecessary repeat imaging also drives cost without a clear benefit when nothing about the patient's clinical picture has changed. The appropriateness framework exists specifically to weigh these risks against expected benefit on a case-by-case basis, rather than applying a blanket schedule to everyone.

What this doesn't mean

  • This isn't a blanket "never repeat" rule. Some patients — for instance, those with known non-obstructive plaque who need monitoring, or specific post-procedure situations — do have a legitimate case for scheduled follow-up imaging, determined individually.
  • AI-QCT's added prognostic value is still being established. Published reviews note that while AI-based plaque quantification correlates well with invasive imaging, its incremental benefit over existing risk tools, and clinically meaningful thresholds for detecting real plaque progression over time, are still active areas of validation.

The verdict

Both halves of this question hold up against current evidence — with one honest gap in what could actually be verified. This file is stamped Mostly Supported. Both underlying premises hold up: AI-assisted coronary CT angiography is real and increasingly standard, and current multi-society appropriate use criteria do not support routine annual repeat testing without a clinical reason.
