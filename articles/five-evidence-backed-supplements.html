The 5 Supplements With Real Evidence Behind Them, According to a New Position Statement

A follow-up to File No. 0006: out of a $400B+ industry mostly built on marketing, these five categories actually have the data to back them up.

Evidence typePosition statement (evidence-graded)
Evidence strengthStrong

This file is a direct sequel to File No. 0006 on the supplement industry's size and risk profile. The claim here is narrower and more testable: that five specific supplement categories are backed by real scientific evidence for athletic performance, rather than marketing.

The claim traces back to the same source as the last file — the National Athletic Trainers' Association's August 2026 position statement on evaluating dietary supplements. Checking it category by category, the claim holds up closely.

1. Caffeine

The most consistently supported ergogenic aid in the sports nutrition literature. The position statement credits it with measurable improvements in endurance, strength, power output, reaction time, and perceived effort — benefits that show up across a wide range of sports and doses.

2. Creatine monohydrate

Arguably the most-studied sports supplement in existence. Strong evidence supports improvements in high-intensity exercise capacity, strength, and hypertrophy (muscle growth) with regular use — this is one of the few supplement claims where the evidence base is genuinely deep rather than a handful of small trials.

3. Beta-alanine

Works by buffering acid buildup in muscle during intense effort — which is what the video refers to as delaying "muscle acidosis." The evidence specifically supports benefits during high-intensity efforts lasting roughly 30 seconds to 10 minutes, making it more sport-specific than caffeine or creatine.

4. Dietary nitrate

Found in beetroot juice and leafy greens, dietary nitrate works through the nitrate–nitrite–nitric oxide pathway, widening blood vessels and improving blood flow. The position statement supports its role in improving exercise capacity and endurance-related performance specifically.

5. Protein

Not framed as inherently magic — the position statement's support is conditional: protein powders, bars, and drinks are useful specifically when they close a real gap in an athlete's dietary protein intake, not as a default addition regardless of diet. This lines up with the statement's broader "food-first, but not food-only" philosophy.

The caveat that matters most

The position statement is explicit that this list isn't a green light to start stacking supplements. Its guidance calls for individual needs assessment before recommending any supplement — what works for a sprinter's 30-second effort isn't the same as what a marathon runner needs, and someone with no dietary protein gap gains nothing from a protein powder they don't need.

The verdict

All five supplements genuinely belong on this list — the only overreach is in how confidently the claim states it. This file is stamped Mostly Supported rather than fully Supported. All five supplements named match categories the NATA position statement backs with evidence, and the specific mechanisms described (acid buffering for beta-alanine, the nitrate–nitrite–NO pathway for dietary nitrate) are accurately represented. The precision gap: the claim's word "proven" overstates the source's own language, which grades this as "strong evidence," not proof — and for protein specifically, the support is conditional on having an actual dietary gap, not universal.

This article is for informational purposes only and does not constitute medical advice. Individual supplement needs vary by sport, training load, and diet — talk to a doctor or registered dietitian before adding any supplement to your routine.
