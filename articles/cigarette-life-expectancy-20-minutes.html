Does One Cigarette Really Cost 20 Minutes of Life, Not 11?

The famous "11 minutes" estimate really has been updated — and the new numbers in the post match the real analysis almost exactly.

Evidence typeAnalytical editorial (recalculated estimate from population data)
Evidence strengthModerate3/5 ●●●○○

The claim

A post marking World No Tobacco Day says the old, widely repeated estimate that a single cigarette costs 11 minutes of life is outdated, and that updated science puts the real figure at 20 minutes per cigarette on average — 17 minutes for men, 22 minutes for women — meaning a pack-a-day smoker permanently loses about 7 hours from that single day.

Original source

The post cites: "Jackson, S.E., Jarvis, M.J., & West, R. (2024). 'The price of a cigarette: An updated assessment of the impact of smoking on life expectancy.' Addiction."

Scientific source

This is real and precisely cited. Sarah E. Jackson, Martin J. Jarvis, and Robert West, all from University College London's Department of Behavioural Science and Health, published "The price of a cigarette: 20 minutes of life?" in Addiction, DOI 10.1111/add.16757. It was published online December 29, 2024 and appeared in the print May 2025 issue (volume 120, issue 5) — which is why the post's "(2024)" citation is defensible as the online publication date, even though the formal journal issue is 2025.

The exact figures — 20 minutes overall, 17 for men, 22 for women — are the paper's own headline numbers, calculated precisely as stated in the post.

What kind of study this actually is

This is worth being precise about: the paper is an editorial/analytical piece, not a new primary study with a fresh patient cohort. Jackson, Jarvis, and West recalculated the estimate by updating the methodology behind a well-known 2000 BMJ analysis (which produced the original "11 minutes" figure) using more recent Health Survey for England data on smoking patterns and cotinine — a biomarker of nicotine exposure. The post accurately describes this as an update to the older estimate, which is exactly what it is.

Why the number changed

The increase from 11 to 20 minutes doesn't mean cigarettes have become more dangerous. It largely reflects that smokers today, on average, smoke fewer cigarettes per day than in 2000 — and the researchers found no corresponding drop in toxicant exposure per cigarette, meaning each individual cigarette accounts for a larger share of a smoker's overall risk than it used to, even though total daily risk may have shifted with reduced overall consumption.

The verdict

This file is stamped Supported. Every specific number in the post — 20 minutes overall, 17 for men, 22 for women — matches the real UCL analysis exactly, the authors and journal are correctly identified, and the post accurately frames this as an update to the older 11-minutes estimate rather than presenting it as an entirely new discovery. This is one of the more precisely accurate files in this archive.
