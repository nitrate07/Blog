Is BMI Actually Useless for Heart Risk? What the New Waist-to-Hip Study Found

A viral claim said belly fat threatens your heart more than your BMI shows. This time, the research backs it — almost exactly.

For most of modern medicine, body mass index (BMI) — weight divided by height squared — has been the default shorthand for "how worried should I be." A viral video argues that's a mistake: that where your fat sits, not just how much of it there is, is what actually threatens your heart.

That claim landed at an unusually good moment to check it. On August 11, 2026, the Journal of the American College of Cardiology (JACC) published a large cross-cohort study built specifically to test whether waist circumference and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) reveal cardiovascular risk that BMI misses.

What the study actually did

Researchers from Johns Hopkins' Ciccarone Center for the Prevention of Cardiovascular Disease, working with the Cross-Cohort Collaboration, tracked nine different cardiovascular outcomes across a large pooled population, comparing BMI alone against BMI combined with waist circumference and waist-to-hip ratio.

The headline finding

People with a "normal" BMI who also had a high waist-to-hip ratio showed up to a 50% higher risk across several cardiovascular outcomes, compared to normal-BMI people with a low WHR. In plain terms: a healthy-looking BMI can hide real cardiovascular risk if fat is concentrated around the abdomen.

The reverse pattern also showed up. Some people classified as having obesity by BMI, but with lower waist circumference, showed cardiovascular risk close to normal-weight, low-waist-circumference peers.

Why fat location matters biologically

The video's explanation lines up with established physiology. Fat stored just under the skin (subcutaneous fat) is relatively metabolically passive. Visceral fat — the fat packed around internal organs, which drives up waist circumference and waist-to-hip ratio — behaves differently: it's more metabolically active and is linked to local, low-grade inflammation that contributes to atherosclerosis and cardiovascular disease over time.

That's a well-established mechanism, not a new or fringe claim — this study adds fresh, large-scale outcome data on top of it.

How to actually measure it

  • Waist circumference: measured at the narrowest point of the torso, without clothing. Elevated risk starts around 35 inches (88 cm) for women and 40 inches (102 cm) for men.
  • Waist-to-hip ratio: waist measurement divided by hip measurement (widest point). A healthy ratio is roughly 0.85 or below for women, 0.90 or below for men.

The verdict

This file gets a rare stamp: Verified. The claim in the video — that BMI alone can miss real cardiovascular risk, and that waist-to-hip ratio catches some of what it misses — matches what a major, brand-new peer-reviewed study found almost exactly. The one caveat worth keeping: this reclassifies risk, it doesn't replace a full cardiovascular workup, and the study's authors themselves frame it as an addition to BMI, not a full replacement for it.
