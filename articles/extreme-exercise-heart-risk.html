Can Extreme Exercise Actually Damage Your Heart? What the Cardiology Evidence Shows

Not "exercise is bad" — a much narrower, well-supported claim about chronic extreme volume, and exactly who it puts at risk.

Evidence typeProspective cohort study + meta-analysis
Evidence strengthModerate

Sedentary behavior is genuinely one of the most well-documented health risks in modern life, and this file doesn't dispute that. The claim under review here is narrower and easy to strip of its nuance if repeated carelessly: that at the extreme end — years of very high-volume endurance training — exercise can place chronic strain on the heart that moderate exercise doesn't produce.

What "extreme" means here

This isn't about recreational runners or people hitting the gym a few times a week. The relevant research population is lifelong endurance athletes: marathoners, ultramarathoners, ironman-distance triathletes, and long-distance cyclists, typically with years to decades of sustained high-volume training.

The coronary calcium finding

A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis in the International Journal of Cardiology confirmed a pattern seen in earlier research: endurance athletes are more likely to have coronary artery calcification than sedentary people — but importantly, they are not more likely to have the unstable, high-risk plaques that actually trigger heart attacks. The calcification found in athletes tends to be more stable. The review's authors note that clear threshold values for "how much is too much" still aren't established.

The right ventricle and rhythm risk

Chronic extreme-endurance training has been linked in the sports cardiology literature to transient right ventricular strain after events like marathons and ironman races — measurable but temporary reductions in right ventricular function that typically normalize within about a week. Over years of repeated strain, this process has been proposed as a contributor to patchy scarring (fibrosis) in some athletes' heart tissue, particularly in the atria and right ventricle.

A study published in January 2026 in the European Journal of Preventive Cardiology — led by researchers at the University of Leeds — put this to a direct test. Researchers followed 106 healthy male endurance runners and cyclists over 50 using wearables and implantable heart monitors. About one in four experienced episodes of ventricular tachycardia, a fast heart rhythm that can become dangerous if sustained — and three in four of those athletes showed evidence of myocardial scarring.

The crucial nuance

This is where the claim needs to stay precise. The study's lead author was explicit: exercise was only linked to abnormal rhythm risk in athletes who already had underlying heart scarring — and athletes who developed rhythm problems were not training harder or more than those who didn't. In the researchers' own framing, exercise appears to act as a trigger in already-vulnerable hearts, not a direct cause of the scarring itself.

What the guidelines actually recommend

Major health authorities, including the WHO and American Heart Association, recommend roughly 150–300 minutes of moderate-intensity exercise (or 75–150 minutes vigorous) per week for general cardiovascular benefit — well below the training volumes seen in the lifelong extreme-endurance athletes this research focuses on. For the vast majority of people, the relevant risk isn't overtraining; it's not moving enough.

The verdict

This file is stamped Mostly Supported rather than fully Supported. Chronic extreme endurance exercise is associated with real, measurable cardiac changes — coronary calcification, transient right ventricular strain, and in some cases myocardial scarring — and a fresh 2026 study links that scarring to arrhythmia risk in older male athletes specifically. The precision gap: the arrhythmia risk itself was concentrated entirely in the subgroup with pre-existing scarring, not in extreme-endurance athletes generally — so the claim is accurate at the population level (real strain occurs) but the scariest specific risk (dangerous rhythm problems) applies to a narrower group than "most people who exercise a lot" might suggest.

This article is for informational purposes only and does not constitute medical advice. Long-time endurance athletes over 50, especially those with unexplained dizziness, palpitations, or breathlessness, should talk to a cardiologist about screening.
