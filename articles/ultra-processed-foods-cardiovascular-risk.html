Do Ultra-Processed Foods Really Raise Heart Death Risk by 65%?

This file covers a two-part video series citing the same source. The statement itself is real, current, and genuinely significant — and it's more careful about what it can and can't prove than the raw percentages alone suggest.

Evidence typeMulti-society clinical consensus statement (observational evidence base)
Evidence strengthHigh4/5 ●●●●○

The claim

Two related posts draw on the same new European Society of Cardiology statement on ultra-processed foods (UPF). One walks through practical label-reading advice — spotting industrial additives like maltodextrin and hydrogenated oils, questioning products marketed as "sugar-free," and noting that food which never spoils often signals heavy processing rather than freshness. The other cites specific figures: a 19% higher risk of cardiovascular disease, a 13% higher risk of atrial fibrillation, and a 65% higher risk of cardiovascular death among the highest UPF consumers, framing packaged food as measurably shortening life even in people at a normal weight.

Original source

Both posts cite: "Guasti, L., Bonaccio, M., et al. 'Ultra-processed foods, lifestyle management, and cardiovascular diseases: A clinical consensus statement of the ESC Council for Cardiology Practice and the EAPC.' European Heart Journal (2026)."

Scientific source

This is real and very current. Led by Luigina Guasti and Marialaura Bonaccio, this ESC Council for Cardiology Practice and European Association of Preventive Cardiology consensus statement was published in the European Heart Journal on May 6, 2026 (DOI: 10.1093/eurheartj/ehag226). The statement's own accompanying press materials confirm it links UPF consumption to obesity, diabetes, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, and cardiovascular death, and calls on cardiologists to routinely discuss UPF intake with patients.

What the statement's own authors emphasize

This is the detail worth weighing carefully: in the ESC's own press release, co-author Dr. Marialaura Bonaccio is quoted directly acknowledging the evidence base's real limits — the associations, in her words, are "consistent and biologically plausible," and the statement's authors explicitly caution that most of the underlying research is observational, with few long-term intervention trials. That's a meaningfully different confidence level than presenting a flat "65% higher death risk" figure without that context — it means we know UPF-heavy diets and worse cardiovascular outcomes travel together consistently across large populations, but a trial directly testing whether reducing UPFs improves outcomes hasn't been done at scale yet, which is exactly why the authors are calling for one.

What holds up

  • The practical label-reading guidance — watching for maltodextrin, hydrogenated oils, emulsifiers, and treating "doesn't spoil" as a processing signal — is a reasonable, low-risk translation of the statement's general concern into everyday advice.
  • The core relationship between UPF intake and worse cardiovascular outcomes is genuinely well-documented across large, diverse populations, per the statement's own summary.

The verdict

This file is stamped Mostly Supported. The ESC statement is real, current, and its general findings — UPFs linked to obesity, hypertension, and cardiovascular death — are accurately represented. The precision gap: presenting specific percentage figures as flatly "official scientific" numbers understates the observational nature of the evidence, a limitation the statement's own co-authors were explicit about in their own public comments.
