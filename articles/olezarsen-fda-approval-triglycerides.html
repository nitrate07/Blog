Did a New Drug Really Just Get FDA Approval for Severe High Triglycerides?

Unlike most files here, this one isn't correcting an exaggeration — the claim is a straightforward regulatory fact, and it checks out almost exactly.

Evidence typeFDA approval + Phase 3 RCTs
Evidence strengthStrong

The claim here is different from most files in this archive: it's not a viral oversimplification of a study, it's a direct news claim — a specific drug, for a specific condition, got FDA approval. That kind of claim is unusually easy to check, because there's an authoritative single source: the FDA itself.

The claim

The video states that for severe hypertriglyceridemia (triglycerides above 500 mg/dL), a new drug called olezarsen has received FDA approval — framed as the start of a new treatment era for this condition.

What actually happened

On June 24, 2026, the FDA approved Tryngolza (olezarsen), made by Ionis Pharmaceuticals, as an add-on to diet to lower triglycerides and reduce the risk of acute pancreatitis in adults with severe hypertriglyceridemia (sHTG) — defined as fasting triglycerides at or above 500 mg/dL. The approval landed ahead of its June 30 target decision date. This makes olezarsen the first drug specifically shown to reduce acute pancreatitis risk in this patient population, according to the FDA's own announcement.

Severe hypertriglyceridemia carries a real, serious risk: acute pancreatitis, sudden pancreatic inflammation that can cause severe pain, repeated hospitalization, and in some cases permanent organ damage or death. Normal triglycerides are under 150 mg/dL, so sHTG represents triglyceride levels more than three times that threshold.

How the drug works

Olezarsen is an antisense oligonucleotide — a lab-made strand of genetic material designed to reduce production of a specific protein, in this case apolipoprotein C-III (apoC-III). ApoC-III normally slows the clearance of triglyceride-rich particles from the blood. By suppressing it, olezarsen allows the liver and an enzyme called lipoprotein lipase to clear triglycerides faster. It's self-administered once a month via a subcutaneous autoinjector.

The trial data behind the approval

The approval was based on two Phase 3 randomized controlled trials, CORE (617 participants) and CORE2 (446 participants). Key results at 6 months, sustained through 12 months:

OutcomeResult
Triglyceride reduction (50 mg dose)~49–63% vs. placebo
Triglyceride reduction (80 mg dose)~55–72% vs. placebo
Patients reaching triglycerides <500 mg/dL86%
Acute pancreatitis eventsRate ratio 0.15 vs. placebo (85% relative reduction)

Olezarsen isn't entirely new to the market — it was first approved in December 2024 for familial chylomicronemia syndrome (FCS), a rare genetic form of severe hypertriglyceridemia. This 2026 approval expands its use to the much larger general sHTG population, which Ionis estimates at more than 3 million people in the US.

What this doesn't mean

  • It's not risk-free. The FDA label and trial data note injection-site reactions and liver enzyme increases as tolerability considerations that require monitoring.
  • It's an add-on to diet, not a replacement for it. The approval specifically frames olezarsen as adjunct therapy alongside dietary management, not a standalone fix.
  • Long-term outcomes beyond 12 months aren't yet established in the same depth as the 6- and 12-month trial data presented here.
  • This is for a specific, severe condition — triglycerides at or above 500 mg/dL — not the much larger population with moderately elevated triglycerides.

The verdict

This file is stamped Supported. Every specific element of the claim checks out against the FDA's own June 24, 2026 announcement: the drug name, the approval, the specific condition and triglyceride threshold, and the framing of it as the first therapy shown to reduce pancreatitis risk in this population. This is one of the more straightforwardly accurate claims filed here — a real regulatory event, described correctly.
