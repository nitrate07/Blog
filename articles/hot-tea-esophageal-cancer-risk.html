Does Drinking Tea Above 60°C Really Raise Cancer Risk by 90%?

A precise, well-established finding from a study that measured tea temperature directly rather than relying on memory or perception.

Evidence typeLarge prospective cohort study
Evidence strengthHigh4/5 ●●●●○

The claim

A post argues that "I drink my tea scalding hot" is a habit worth reconsidering. It cites a large study following 50,000 people for 10 years, finding that people who regularly drank tea above 60°C had a 90% higher risk of esophageal cancer. It frames the mechanism as repeated thermal injury to the esophageal lining causing chronic inflammation, and offers a simple fix: let tea sit for 4-6 minutes after pouring before drinking it.

Original source

The post cites: "Islami, F., et al. (2019). A prospective study of tea drinking temperature and risk of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma. International Journal of Cancer, 146(1), 18-25."

Scientific source

This is a real, well-known, and precisely cited study. Led by Dr. Farhad Islami of the American Cancer Society, it followed 50,045 people aged 40-75 in northeastern Iran for a median of 10 years, and — importantly — measured tea temperature directly with a thermometer at baseline rather than relying on people's subjective sense of "hot" or "very hot," which had limited earlier research on this topic. During follow-up, 317 new esophageal cancer cases were identified.

What the study actually found

Compared to drinking less than 700 mL of tea per day at under 60°C, drinking 700 mL or more per day at 60°C or higher was associated with a 90% higher risk of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma — the specific cancer subtype the study examined. This is the study's own headline finding, and it matches the post's number exactly. Independent experts described the underlying research design as methodologically strong: temperature measurements were done well, and the number of cancer cases was sufficient to detect a real effect if one exists.

What this doesn't mean

  • It's an association from an observational cohort, not a controlled experiment — though for a question like this (you can't ethically randomize people to drink scalding tea for years), a large, well-measured prospective cohort is close to the strongest evidence realistically obtainable.
  • The 90% figure applies specifically to squamous cell carcinoma, the esophageal cancer subtype more strongly linked to thermal and chemical irritation, not to all esophageal cancers broadly.
  • This doesn't mean any warm beverage is dangerous — the finding is specifically about drinking large volumes at temperatures of 60°C and above, not tea or coffee at typical drinking temperatures.

The verdict

This file is stamped Supported. Every specific detail in the post — the 50,000-person cohort, the 10-year follow-up, the 60°C threshold, and the 90% increased risk — matches the real study precisely, and the study itself is a methodologically strong, frequently cited piece of cancer epidemiology.
