Is Excess Sodium Really the #1 Dietary Killer for Hearts Worldwide?

The headline figures are real. The claim slightly compresses a three-factor finding into a single-villain story.

Evidence typeGlobal burden-of-disease modeling study
Evidence strengthHigh4/5 ●●●●○

The claim

A post cites a large 2026 study covering 204 countries, arguing that excess sodium is the single biggest dietary contributor to cardiovascular deaths worldwide, that even modest sodium restriction (1-2 grams per day) is one of the most effective known interventions against stroke deaths at a population level, and that "natural" or "mineral-rich" salts marketed as healthier alternatives don't change this — the artery doesn't care about the salt's name, only the sodium and the inflammation it drives.

Original source

The post cites: "The Innovation Nutrition, 2026 Vol. 1, No. 1, 100015. 'Cardiovascular diseases burden attributable to dietary risk factors in 204 countries and territories.'"

Scientific source

This is real and current. The study — by Mei, Wang, Xu, and colleagues, published in The Innovation Nutrition (DOI: 10.59717/j.xinn-nutri.2026.100015) — found that dietary risk factors were responsible for 5.91 million cardiovascular deaths globally in 2023, and identified a "top three" list of dietary drivers: high sodium intake, low fruit consumption, and inadequate whole grain intake. High sodium intake is listed first among these three in the study's own framing, and the researchers specifically call for policy measures like mandatory sodium labeling and food reformulation in high-population countries.

What the data actually shows

  • 5.91 million cardiovascular deaths in 2023 were attributable to dietary risk factors overall — this is the study's real, current headline figure, matching the post exactly.
  • High sodium intake was identified as the leading single dietary driver among the three factors examined, which supports the post's "sodium is culprit #1" framing.
  • The study calls for context-specific policy responses — structural interventions like sodium labeling for large countries, and improved access to fresh produce in smaller, more vulnerable economies.

What this doesn't mean

  • The 5.9 million figure reflects the combined burden of multiple dietary risk factors, not sodium alone. Sodium tops the list, but low fruit and low whole-grain intake are also named as major contributors to the same total — the post's framing of sodium as "the" cause of the full 5.9 million slightly compresses a three-factor finding into a single-villain narrative.
  • This is a burden-of-disease modeling study, which estimates population-level impact using existing epidemiological and dietary intake data — it's a different evidence type from a randomized sodium-reduction trial testing the effect directly, though it draws on a large body of underlying evidence.

The verdict

This file is stamped Mostly Supported. The study is real, current, and correctly cited, and its headline finding — 5.91 million cardiovascular deaths in 2023 attributable to diet, with sodium as the top single driver — matches the post closely. The precision gap: the 5.9 million figure is the combined burden of three dietary risk factors, and framing sodium alone as responsible for the entire number slightly overstates its individual share.
