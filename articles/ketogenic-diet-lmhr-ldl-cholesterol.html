Can a Keto Diet Push LDL to 300+ in Lean, Healthy People?

The phenomenon is real and the paper is correctly cited. The precision gap is in evidence type — and in exactly who this happens to.

Evidence typeNarrative review
Evidence strengthModerate3/5 ●●●○○

The claim

A post highlights an underrecognized risk of ketogenic diets: in lean, metabolically healthy people, carbohydrate restriction can trigger a dramatic LDL cholesterol spike — sometimes to 200, 300, or higher — described as a possible "new fuel strategy" rather than automatically a heart-attack warning sign. It references a 2026 review in the Journal of the American Heart Association covering what's known as the Lean Mass Hyper-Responder (LMHR) phenotype: why cholesterol rises more in lean people on keto, the "Lipid Energy Model" explaining LDL as a fuel-carrier rather than waste, and whether a small dietary adjustment ("carb rescue") can bring levels back down.

Original source

The post cites: "DiMattia, Z.S., & Petersen, K.S. (2026). Ketogenic Diets and Low-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol in Adults With Normal Weight: An Emerging Clinical Challenge. Journal of the American Heart Association."

Scientific source

This is real and precisely cited. Zachary S. DiMattia and Kristina S. Petersen published this exact paper in JAHA on April 28, 2026 (DOI: 10.1161/JAHA.125.048903). The LMHR phenotype — lean, metabolically healthy people with a low triglyceride-to-HDL ratio who show unusually large LDL increases on very-low-carbohydrate diets — is a genuinely recognized and increasingly studied pattern in the cardiology literature, described in multiple independent papers beyond this one, including case reports of LDL levels exceeding 400-500 mg/dL after starting keto.

What kind of evidence this is

This is worth being precise about: the JAHA paper is a review article, not a new clinical trial with its own patient cohort. A review's value is in synthesizing existing evidence and proposing a clinical framework — it's a lower rung on the evidence hierarchy than a controlled trial, even when, as here, it's published in a well-regarded journal. Independent commentary on the paper has noted this directly, along with a related observation: the LDL-spike phenomenon on ketogenic diets isn't exclusively confined to lean or normal-weight people, even though "lean" is in the phenotype's name — some overweight and obese individuals on ketogenic diets show similar patterns, which nuances the post's framing of this as a distinctly lean-person issue.

Limitations

  • The "Lipid Energy Model" is a proposed explanatory framework, not an established mechanism with the same evidentiary weight as, say, a well-replicated randomized trial. It's a genuine, actively discussed hypothesis in this research area, not something invented for the post, but it remains under active investigation and debate.
  • Long-term cardiovascular outcome data for LMHR individuals specifically is still limited — most current evidence describes the cholesterol pattern itself, not definitive long-term heart-attack or stroke risk figures for this specific subgroup.
  • The post itself includes an appropriate disclaimer noting this isn't medical advice and that people should consult a doctor before making major dietary changes — a responsible inclusion given the topic.

The verdict

This file is stamped Mostly Supported. The underlying phenomenon, the paper, its authors, and its publication details are all accurately represented. The precision gap: this is a review synthesizing existing evidence rather than new primary data, and the "lean people specifically" framing is somewhat narrower than what independent analysis of the broader LMHR literature suggests.
