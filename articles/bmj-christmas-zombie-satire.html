Is the "Zombie Infection" Study From a Real Medical Journal?

Read this one first: nothing here is real epidemiological guidance about zombies. The file is about whether a satire tradition was represented honestly — not about zombie biology.

Evidence typeSatirical journal special issue (self-disclosed as humor)
Evidence strengthNot applicable — satire, not a scientific claim
Before anything else: zombies are fictional. This file does not evaluate any claim about zombie biology, treatment, or epidemiology as if it were real, because none of it is. What's actually being checked here is narrower and more mundane: is the cited journal real, is the humor issue tradition real, and did the post accurately tell its audience this was satire rather than passing it off as genuine medical research.

The claim

A post asks whether humanity could survive a zombie outbreak, and says the British Medical Journal — a real, long-established, highly regarded medical journal — published an official "infection guide" examining this scenario using rigorous scientific methodology. It explicitly frames this as coming from the BMJ's "Christmas" issue, which it describes as an annual tradition of examining humorous topics with strict scientific methodology, and repeatedly signals the content as playful ("mizahi") throughout the post.

Original source

The post cites: "Journal: British Medical Journal (BMJ) Christmas Edition. Article: Zombie infections: epidemiology, treatment, and prevention. Author: Dr. Tara C. Smith (Kent State University). Citation: BMJ 2015;351:h6423."

Scientific source

Both halves of this claim are real. The BMJ's Christmas issue is a genuine, decades-long editorial tradition: every December, the journal publishes a special edition of articles that apply real scientific rigor and peer review to deliberately light-hearted or absurd topics, precisely as the post describes it. This is a well-known, celebrated tradition in medical publishing circles, not something invented for this post.

What the post gets right that matters most

The post does not present zombie epidemiology as real. It explicitly names the source as the BMJ's humor-focused Christmas edition, and repeats that framing multiple times rather than burying it. That distinction is the entire substance of what this file is checking: whether the underlying source was accurately represented as satire, not smuggled in as if it were ordinary peer-reviewed medical science. On that specific, narrow question, the post is transparent.

What this doesn't mean

  • It doesn't mean anything about zombies is scientifically true — that's the entire premise of a satire issue, and this file isn't evaluating fictional biology.
  • It doesn't mean every "official-sounding" citation from a real journal is automatically serious. This is exactly the kind of case where checking the source matters: the citation format looks like a normal academic reference, but the content behind it is intentionally not meant to be read as literal guidance.

The verdict

This file is stamped Supported. Both the existence of a real BMJ Christmas humor tradition and the post's accurate, repeated disclosure that this is satire check out. Unlike most files in this archive, the underlying "claim" isn't a scientific assertion to test — it's a claim about the nature and honesty of a source, and on that question, the post holds up.
