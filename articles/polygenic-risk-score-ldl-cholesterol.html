Does Your Genetic Score Mean an LDL of 100 Is as Risky as 190?

The risk comparison is a real finding. What's missing from the post is what kind of publication this actually is, and who paid for it.

Evidence typeLetter to the editor (industry-funded, commenting on UK Biobank data)
Evidence strengthModerate3/5 ●●●○○

The claim

A post argues that lab results showing LDL cholesterol around 100-110 mg/dL shouldn't automatically feel reassuring. It cites a new JACC Advances paper arguing that coronary heart disease risk depends not just on how much cholesterol someone has, but how genetically susceptible their arteries are to it — measured by a Polygenic Risk Score (PRS). It claims that someone with a high PRS and an LDL of 100 can carry the same heart-attack risk as someone with low genetic risk and an LDL of 190, and calls for genetic testing in people with a family history of early heart attacks.

Original source

The post cites: "Article: Joint Consideration of LDL-C and Polygenic Risk for Coronary Heart Disease Risk Assessment. Authors: Carlos Iribarren, MD, MPH, PhD, et al. Journal: JACC Advances. Date: 2025. DOI: 10.1016/j.jacadv.2025.102228."

Scientific source

This citation is real, and the DOI, authors, and journal all match exactly. But it's worth being precise about what kind of publication this is: it's a "To the Editor" letter — "Correcting Misaligned Risk Comparisons Between LDL-C Levels and Polygenic Risk Scores" — not a new original study with its own patient cohort. The letter is itself commenting on and re-analyzing risk figures from an earlier UK Biobank study.

The specific comparison the post describes does appear in the letter: individuals with high PRS and LDL-C 130 to under 160 mg/dL showed a similar elevated risk (hazard ratio 2.2) to those with intermediate PRS and LDL 190 or higher (hazard ratio 2.3) — and the letter states that risk was significantly elevated even at LDL-C 100 mg/dL and above in people with high polygenic risk.

What the post doesn't mention

  • This is a letter, not a primary study. Its scientific weight comes from re-analyzing existing UK Biobank data, not from new patient recruitment or original data collection.
  • The letter discloses a funding conflict the post doesn't mention: the underlying work was funded by a grant from GENinCode, PLC — a company that commercially sells polygenic risk score genetic tests. One of the letter's authors has also received a research grant from the same company. That doesn't make the numbers wrong, but a call to action recommending more PRS testing, funded by a company that sells PRS tests, is exactly the kind of detail readers deserve to know.

What holds up

The core scientific idea — that polygenic background can meaningfully modulate the cardiovascular risk carried by a given LDL level — is a genuine and active area of cardiovascular genetics research, discussed in other, independently funded work as well, including a related multi-ethnic cohort study by overlapping authors published in the European Journal of Preventive Cardiology.

The verdict

This file is stamped Partly Supported. The specific risk comparison in the post is accurately drawn from a real, correctly cited source. What pulls the file down: the source is a letter re-analyzing someone else's data rather than new original research, and it carries a real, undisclosed-in-the-post financial conflict of interest with a company that sells the exact test being recommended.

This article is for informational purposes only and does not constitute medical advice. Whether polygenic risk testing is appropriate for you is a decision to make with a cardiologist or genetic counselor.
