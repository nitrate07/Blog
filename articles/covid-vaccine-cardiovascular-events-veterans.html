Does the Updated COVID-19 Vaccine Really Cut Major Heart Events by 38%?

The headline number is real and precisely cited. The vivid story about how it works — a virus quietly damaging arteries in people who feel fine — reaches further than this particular study can prove.

Evidence typeCohort study (target-trial emulation)
Evidence strengthHigh4/5 ●●●●○

The claim

A post argues that on top of whatever protection a flu shot provides, getting the updated COVID-19 vaccine alongside it lowers the risk of major cardiac events — heart attack, stroke, heart failure hospitalization — by 37.7%, and that this reflects a hidden vascular threat from the virus itself, even in people who never felt sick.

Original source

The claim comes from an Instagram post citing "Cai M, Xie Y, Al-Aly Z. 2024-2025 COVID-19 Vaccine and Major Adverse Cardiovascular Events Among US Veterans. JAMA Intern Med. Published online June 15, 2026," with the DOI included directly in the caption.

Scientific source

This is a real, very recent study. Miao Cai, Yan Xie, and Ziyad Al-Aly of the VA St. Louis Health Care System published this exact paper in JAMA Internal Medicine, online June 15, 2026 (DOI: 10.1001/jamainternmed.2026.1929). It's a cohort study using a target-trial emulation design — a rigorous observational method built to approximate a randomized trial using real-world health records — drawing on more than one million US veterans who received influenza vaccination at VA facilities in 2024, comparing those who also received the updated COVID-19 vaccine to those who received the flu shot alone.

What the data actually shows

OutcomeVaccine effectiveness
COVID-associated MACE (composite endpoint)37.7%
COVID-associated cardiovascular death57.9%
COVID-associated myocardial infarction38.5%
COVID-associated heart failure hospitalization41.9%

The 37.7% figure in the post matches the study's own headline result for the composite MACE endpoint exactly. Protection was most pronounced in veterans 75 and older and those with existing cardiovascular disease, kidney disease, or diabetes — also consistent with the post's framing that certain groups benefit more. Independent coverage from STAT News described the topline effect as "roughly 38% lower risk," matching the same figure.

Limitations

  • It's observational, not a randomized trial. Target-trial emulation is a rigorous design for real-world data, but it still can't fully rule out that vaccinated and unvaccinated veterans differed in other health-seeking behaviors that also affect cardiac risk.
  • The mechanism story outruns this specific study. The post describes COVID as a "sneaky vascular enemy" causing silent endothelial damage and plaque rupture even in asymptomatic infection. That mechanism is biologically plausible and discussed elsewhere in the cardiology literature, but this particular paper measured vaccine effectiveness against clinical outcomes — it didn't image arteries or directly demonstrate that specific mechanism in this cohort.
  • The comparison group still got a flu shot. This is actually a methodological strength worth naming explicitly: the study didn't compare vaccinated people to people who avoid all vaccines, which would confound the result with general health-seeking behavior. Both study groups were vaccine-accepting; only the COVID-19 vaccine varied.
  • The population is veterans, skewed older and male. Results may not generalize identically to a younger, more sex-balanced general population.

The verdict

This file is stamped Mostly Supported. The core number — a 37.7% reduction in COVID-associated major cardiovascular events — is accurately quoted from a real, very recent JAMA Internal Medicine study, and the study's comparator design (flu-vaccine-only, not vaccine-avoidant) is genuinely one of its methodological strengths. The precision gap: the vivid mechanistic narrative about silent endothelial damage in asymptomatic infection is a reasonable extrapolation from broader cardiology research, not something this specific cohort study measured directly.
