Does Rock Salt's "84 Minerals" Make It Harmless? What the Evidence Actually Shows

Goats lick salt stones, and the internet took it as proof that "natural" salt gets a health pass. The chemistry says otherwise.

Evidence typeCompositional analysis + physiology review
Evidence strengthStrong

This file is a little different from the others: instead of checking a fresh claim, it checks a claim that's been circulating for years — that pink Himalayan or rock salt is essentially harmless, or even healthy, because it contains up to 84 trace minerals that "regular" table salt lacks. It's one of the most widely repeated pieces of nutrition folklore online.

What's actually true about the 84 minerals

The number itself isn't fabricated. Spectral analyses of rock salt from mines like Pakistan's Khewra Salt Mine really do detect around 84 distinct elements. But "detected" and "nutritionally meaningful" are very different things.

Rock and pink salts are still roughly 97–98% sodium chloride by weight — nearly identical to table salt. The remaining 2% is where all 84 trace elements live, and most exist in amounts so small they're irrelevant to your diet. To get a nutritionally meaningful dose of potassium from pink salt alone, for example, you'd need to eat roughly 1.7 kg of it — an amount that would deliver a wildly dangerous dose of sodium long before it delivered a useful one of potassium.

The physiology the video actually gets right

Here's the more interesting, well-supported part of the video's argument: human kidneys evolved under conditions of severe sodium scarcity. Classic anthropological work by Eaton and Konner estimated ancestral sodium intake at under 1 gram of salt per day — a tiny fraction of modern consumption — alongside a diet extremely rich in potassium from plants. Estimates of the ancestral sodium-to-potassium intake ratio run as low as 1:10 to 1:16; the typical modern Western diet runs closer to 1:1 or 2:1.

That mismatch matters physiologically. A well-cited 2014 paper in the Journal of the American Society of Hypertension frames this directly: it's not sodium excess or potassium deficiency alone that drives hypertension risk, but the combination — "sodium surfeit and potassium deficit" together. Human kidneys are tuned to conserve sodium tightly and excrete potassium relatively freely, a setup that made sense for 99% of human evolutionary history and works against us now.

Why "natural" doesn't change the sodium math

This is the core of why the popular claim is misleading rather than simply wrong: it's not that rock salt is dangerous — it's that framing it as a health upgrade over table salt, "because minerals," misdirects attention from what actually matters, which is total sodium intake and dietary potassium. A pinch of pink salt on your food changes essentially nothing about your cardiovascular risk compared to the same pinch of table salt.

What actually helps

  • Total sodium intake matters more than salt type. Pink, rock, sea, or table — track how much, not which kind.
  • Potassium is the underused lever. Vegetables, legumes, fruit, and other whole plant foods raise potassium intake in a way no salt swap can.
  • "Natural" and "healthier" aren't the same claim. Less processing doesn't automatically mean lower risk.

The verdict

This file is stamped Misleading. The 84-minerals fact is real but functionally irrelevant at normal intake levels, and the broader claim it's used to support — that rock or pink salt is meaningfully safer for blood pressure than table salt — isn't supported by the evidence. The video's underlying physiology explanation, on the other hand, checks out against well-established research.
