Does Creatine Really Not Harm Your Kidneys?

The cited meta-analysis's actual conclusion reads almost like a direct translation of what the post says.

Evidence typeSystematic review and meta-analysis (19 RCTs)
Evidence strengthHigh4/5 ●●●●○

The claim

A post addresses a common gym fear: that creatine damages the kidneys. It cites a new meta-analysis finding that creatine supplementation raises serum creatinine by an average of about 0.13 mg/dL, but argues this isn't kidney damage — it's a harmless lab artifact from a larger body creatine pool, since eGFR (the actual measure of kidney filtering performance) and urea showed no meaningful change, in both short-term and longer-term use. It also notes scientists' own caveat: current data covers mostly short-to-medium-term use, and longer studies beyond a year are still needed.

Original source

The post cites: "Tsiaras, A., Loufopoulos, G., Theodoridis, X., Liakopoulos, V., Poulia, K.A., & Chourdakis, M. (2026). 'The effect of creatine supplementation on kidney function: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials.' Journal of Renal Nutrition. Advance online publication."

Scientific source

This is a real, current meta-analysis, published in the Journal of Renal Nutrition — the official journal of the National Kidney Foundation's Council on Renal Nutrition — with the exact DOI cited (10.1053/j.jrn.2026.04.010). The authors and institutional affiliations match, including researchers from Aristotle University of Thessaloniki and the Agricultural University of Athens.

The journal's own published conclusion states: creatine supplementation appears related to an increase in serum creatinine, but this is not associated with significant changes in urea concentrations and eGFR, and further long-term RCTs extending beyond one year are needed to assess safety. This is close to a direct match with the post's framing, down to the specific call for longer studies.

What the data actually shows

  • Serum creatinine rises modestly with creatine supplementation — the post's 0.13 mg/dL figure is in the same range as comparable creatine-kidney meta-analyses, which have reported similar small increases.
  • eGFR and urea — the more direct measures of actual kidney filtering function — showed no significant change, which is the central reason researchers interpret the creatinine rise as a metabolic byproduct rather than kidney injury.
  • This held across both shorter and longer supplementation periods within the studies reviewed.

Limitations

  • The review's own authors are explicit that long-term safety beyond one year isn't yet well established — the post accurately preserves this caveat rather than dropping it.
  • This applies to people without pre-existing kidney disease. The post's own closing line makes this distinction — people with a diagnosed chronic kidney condition are a different population than the one this evidence base covers.

The verdict

This file is stamped Supported. The meta-analysis is real, current, correctly cited, and its published conclusion matches the post's claim closely — including the honest caveat about needing longer-term data. This is one of the more precisely accurate claims filed in this archive.

This article is for informational purposes only and does not constitute medical advice. People with existing kidney disease should talk to a doctor before using creatine or any supplement.
